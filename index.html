Opening the Doors

Women Fight for Educational Access

FULL-WIDTH HISTORICAL IMAGE

1837

Oberlin College Opens Its Doors to Women

Oberlin College became the first college in the United States to adopt a policy of admitting women into its regular collegiate program. This challenged the belief that advanced education belonged primarily to men and created a pathway for women to pursue the same level of academic study that had long been reserved for male students. Oberlin set an important precedent for other colleges to expand educational opportunities for women.

(Oberlin College, n.d.)

1849

Elizabeth Blackwell Earns a Medical Degree

Elizabeth Blackwell became the first woman to receive an M.D. degree from an American medical school. Her achievement challenged assumptions about women's intellectual abilities and showed how education could open professions that had traditionally been reserved for men.

(National Library of Medicine, n.d.)

1861

Vassar College Is Founded

Vassar was founded to offer women a liberal arts education comparable to the education available at leading men's colleges. It expanded opportunities at a time when many established universities still refused to admit women.

(Vassar College, n.d.)

1972

Title IX Becomes Law

Title IX prohibited sex discrimination in education programs or activities receiving federal financial assistance. The law strengthened women's educational rights and expanded opportunities across academics, professional programs, athletics, and other areas of school life.

(Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts, n.d.)

Black Women's History

Black Women & Education

1904

Mary McLeod Bethune Opens a School for Black Girls

Education as Resistance

At a time when educational opportunities for Black children—especially Black girls—were severely limited, Mary McLeod Bethune treated education as a pathway to independence and equality. In October 1904, she opened a school in Daytona Beach, Florida, with six students: five girls and her young son. The school grew from a small rented house into an institution that eventually became Bethune-Cookman College. Bethune's work shows that Black women did not only fight for access to existing schools; they also created educational institutions and opportunities of their own.

(National Park Service, 2020)

Key Moments

1892

Anna Julia Cooper Publishes A Voice from the South

Cooper argued that Black women's rights and education were essential to self-determination and racial progress.

(National Park Service, 2024)

1904

Mary McLeod Bethune Opens Her Daytona School

Bethune began with only a handful of students and built an institution dedicated to expanding educational opportunities for Black students.

(National Park Service, 2020)

1954

Mamie Phipps Clark's Research Helps Challenge Segregation

Clark's research on racial identity and the psychological effects of segregation was used as evidence in Brown v. Board of Education.

(National Museum of African American History and Culture, n.d.)

2001

Ruth Simmons Becomes the First Black President of an Ivy League University

Simmons became president of Brown University, breaking another barrier for Black women in educational leadership.

(National Museum of African American History and Culture, n.d.)

Woman to Know

Anna Julia Cooper

Educator · Scholar · Advocate

Born into slavery in 1858, Anna Julia Cooper became an educator, scholar, and pioneering voice for the education of Black women. As a student at St. Augustine's, she successfully petitioned to take courses that had previously been reserved for boys. She later attended Oberlin College and taught at Washington, D.C.'s M Street High School, where she became principal and advocated for Black students to receive rigorous college-preparatory education rather than being limited to vocational training. Cooper's career connected educational access with both racial and gender equality.

(National Park Service, 2024)

Chicana & Latina History

Chicana & Latina Women & Education

April 14, 1947

Gonzalo and Felicitas Mendez, Sylvia Mendez

Segregation & Resistance

Sylvia Mendez was a Mexican American student in California whose family was denied access to the local school designated for white children. Her parents, Gonzalo and Felicitas Mendez, joined other families in challenging the school districts' segregation practices. In 1947, the Ninth Circuit upheld the ruling against the districts, making Mendez v. Westminster an important step toward ending school segregation in California.

(Zinn Education Project, n.d.; Mendez et al. v. Westminster, n.d.)

Key Moments

1830s

Mexicana and Tejana Women Advocate for Schooling

In Texas, Mexicana and Tejana women advocated for Catholic schools and churches to provide educational opportunities for children in their communities.

(Morales, 2019)

1947

Mendez v. Westminster Challenges School Segregation

The Mendez family's case challenged the segregation of Mexican American students in Orange County schools and helped push California toward ending school segregation.

(Facing History & Ourselves, n.d.)

March 1968

Students Lead the East Los Angeles Walkouts

Thousands of students walked out of East Los Angeles schools to protest discriminatory treatment and unequal educational conditions. Student leaders helped bring demands for better schooling into public view.

(Los Angeles Almanac, n.d.)

1976

Martha P. Cotera Publishes Diosa y Hembra

Cotera's book documented the history and heritage of Chicanas in the United States and contributed to efforts to make Chicana history visible in education and scholarship.

(Cotera, 1976)

Woman to Know

Martha P. Cotera

Author · Activist · Educator

Martha P. Cotera documented Chicana history and advocated for Chicana and Mexican American women's experiences to be represented in education. Her 1976 book Diosa y Hembra: The History and Heritage of Chicanas in the U.S. helped make histories that had often been overlooked more visible to students, educators, and scholars.

Native American History

Native American Women & Education

1879–1960s

The Federal Indian Boarding School Era

Assimilation, Resistance & Sovereignty

Beginning in the late 1800s, the U.S. government expanded boarding schools designed to assimilate Native children into white American culture. Students were separated from their families and pressured or forbidden from speaking Native languages, practicing traditions, and wearing traditional clothing. For Native girls, education could provide literacy and opportunity while also threatening cultural identity. Native women increasingly used education, writing, and activism to protect their cultures and advocate for Native sovereignty.

(National Park Service, 2022a; National Park Service, 2022b)

Two Stories of Education

Education as Assimilation

Carlisle Indian Industrial School

Carlisle opened in 1879 and enrolled Native children from more than 140 tribal nations. Students were pressured to abandon their languages, names, clothing, religions, and cultural practices in favor of Anglo-American customs, showing how education could be used as a tool of forced assimilation.

(National Park Service, 2022b)

Education as Resistance

Zitkála-Šá's School Days of an Indian Girl

After experiencing boarding-school education, Zitkála-Šá used writing as a form of resistance. Her autobiographical work exposed the cultural loss Native children experienced in assimilationist schools and used the skills she had learned to preserve Indigenous stories and challenge stereotypes.

(National Park Service, 2022a)

Woman to Know

Zitkála-Šá

Writer · Educator · Activist

Zitkála-Šá, also known as Gertrude Simmons Bonnin, lived from 1876 to 1938. She was a Yankton Dakota writer, educator, musician, and activist. After experiencing assimilationist boarding schools as a child, she later attended college and became a teacher. She used her writing to expose the harms of Native boarding schools while preserving Indigenous stories and traditions. Her life demonstrates how Native women could reclaim education as a source of resistance, cultural preservation, and political advocacy.

(National Park Service, 2022b)

04

Then & Now

How Does This History Shape Schools Today?

Women's access to education has expanded dramatically, but access alone does not guarantee equal treatment, representation, or opportunity. Looking at gender alongside race helps reveal where historical inequities continue to shape students' experiences.

1.15M

Access

About 1.15 million bachelor's degrees were awarded to women in 2022–23, compared with about 817,000 awarded to men. Women now earn the majority of bachelor's degrees in the United States, showing how dramatically formal access has changed. The same NCES data also show differences by race and ethnicity, making an intersectional view of educational access important.

(National Center for Education Statistics, 2024)

~50%

Discipline

Black girls represented 15% of girls in U.S. public schools in 2017–18 but received almost half of suspensions and expulsions given to girls. The GAO also found that Black girls could receive harsher consequences than White girls for similar behaviors, showing how racial and gender bias can still shape who is removed from the classroom.

(U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2024)

35%

Representation

Women made up 35% of the U.S. STEM workforce in 2021 while making up 47% of the employed population. The gap begins before employment: among 2019 high school graduates, only about 6% of females had taken an engineering course, compared with about 18% of males. Equal access to school therefore does not always translate into equal representation in every academic and career pathway.

(National Science Board, 2024; National Center for Education Statistics, n.d.)

$1,479

Opportunity

Higher educational attainment is associated with higher median earnings, but an earnings gap remains within the same broad education category. In 2024, women working full time with a bachelor's degree or higher had median weekly earnings of $1,479, compared with $1,907 for men. The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that these broad comparisons do not control for factors such as occupation, experience, job responsibilities, or specialization.

(U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026)

05

The Work Continues

What Can Educators Do?

Because nearly every educator teaches girls and young women, gender equity is not a specialized issue—it is part of everyday teaching. Educators can examine their own expectations, make women's contributions visible across the curriculum, and actively create pathways into opportunities where girls have historically been underrepresented.

01

Represent

Make women's diverse experiences visible throughout the curriculum.

Represent women from different racial, cultural, and professional backgrounds throughout the year—not only during heritage months or special units. Build women into examples, readings, historical narratives, STEM problems, and classroom displays so students regularly see women as thinkers, leaders, creators, and experts.

(National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020)

02

Listen

Create space for students and communities to tell you what they need.

Avoid assuming that every student experiences school in the same way. Ask students about their experiences, listen when they identify barriers or bias, and include families and communities in conversations about belonging, discipline, opportunity, and support. Listening helps educators respond to students' actual experiences instead of relying on assumptions.

(Patrick et al., 2020)

03

Question

Question your assumptions before they become expectations.

Reflect on whose behavior you interpret as disruptive, who receives the benefit of the doubt, and whether race or gender stereotypes may be shaping disciplinary decisions. Before moving directly to punishment, pause to understand what happened and listen to the student. Educators should also examine who they encourage toward advanced STEM opportunities and whether their expectations reinforce stereotypes about who belongs in those fields.

(U.S. Government Accountability Office, 2024; Patrick et al., 2020; Halpern et al., 2007)

04

Include

Make women's contributions part of the curriculum, not an occasional addition.

Include women from diverse racial and cultural backgrounds in classroom examples, readings, displays, problems, and discussions throughout the year. In STEM classrooms, go beyond putting women on posters: teach their work, connect content to women working in the field, and expose students to female role models who challenge stereotypes about who belongs in STEM.

(National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020; Halpern et al., 2007)

05

Advocate

Create pathways, not just encouragement.

Advocate for concrete opportunities that connect girls with female STEM role models, mentors, career exploration, advanced coursework, and programs that build a sense of belonging in fields where women remain underrepresented. At the school level, educators can also advocate for data-informed and restorative discipline practices that reduce unnecessary exclusion from learning, especially for girls of color.

(National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2020; Halpern et al., 2007; Patrick et al., 2020)
